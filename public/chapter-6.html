Unskilled AttackersUse brute force or simple tools; often crash servers rather than stealthily steal data.Using a "hammer" to crash a server.

SECTION 2: TARGETS & SCOUTING

🔑 TARGETS OF OPPORTUNITY vs SPECIFIC TARGETS

Definition: Attackers either stumble upon a vulnerable target (opportunity) or deliberately plan an attack (specific target).

Target TypeDescriptionExamples
Targets of OpportunityUnsecured networks the attacker stumbles upon.Unsecured Wi-Fi in an executive's home, unsecured wiring closet, rogue AP installed by an employee, employee connecting to free public Wi-Fi.
Specific TargetsThe hacker has a specific goal in mind (e.g., a company, a person, a database).Targeted attack on a corporation, government agency.

Real-World Example:

Think of opportunity like a pickpocket in a crowd — they grab whatever is easy.

Think of a specific target like a heist movie — months of planning to break into one vault.

🔑 SCOUTING FOR A TARGETED ATTACK

Definition: When planning a cyberattack, attackers spend a great deal of time profiling the network and organization, probing for clues.

What Attackers Gather (Layer 2 Information):

InformationWhy It Matters
MAC addressesIdentify devices.
SSIDsNetwork names.
BSSIDsAP identifiers.
Device types deployedWhat hardware is in use.
Authentication use and typeWhat security is in place.
Encryption use and typeWhat encryption is used.
Channels in useWhat channels are occupied.
Default configurationsEasy targets (default passwords, etc.).
Radio interference from Wi-Fi neighborsWhat else is competing for spectrum.

Memory Aid:

"M-S-B-D-A-E-C-D-R" — MAC, SSID, BSSID, Devices, Auth, Encryption, Channels, Defaults, Radio.

Real-World Example:

Think of scouting like a burglar casing a house. They look at the locks, the windows, the alarm system, the neighborhood—everything before they strike.
SECTION 3: PHYSICAL SECURITY & WIRELESS

🔑 PHYSICAL SECURITY AND WIRELESS NETWORKS

Definition: The best way to prevent casual eavesdropping is to control the radiation of the RF signal outside the premises.

Key Points:

  • Assume the internal network is not compromised.
  • Secure physical access to the building or office.
  • Secure internal systems.
  • Shut down all unused switch ports on switches. This prevents users from plugging unauthorized devices into wall sockets.
  • This simple measure alone can prevent attackers or malicious insiders from installing rogue access points.

Real-World Example:

Think of physical security like locking your doors and windows. If you don't, anyone can walk in and plug in a rogue device.

Key Takeaway:

Physical security = first line of defense. Lock unused ports, lock the building, lock the server room.
SECTION 4: SOCIAL ENGINEERING

🔑 SOCIAL ENGINEERING

Definition: The practice of teasing out information from people that should not be shared, to use it to one's advantage.

Key Points:

  • The end user is a significant weakness in any technical system.
  • Phishing is an example of social engineering.
  • Social engineering exploits human tendencies.

Human Tendencies Exploited:

TendencyHow Attackers Use It
People are generally nice and want to help others.Attacker pretends to need help.
People tend to reciprocate favors.Attacker offers a small favor, then asks for a bigger one.
People are curious.Attacker leaves a USB drive labeled "Payroll 2026" for someone to plug in.
People tend to respond to authority figures.Attacker pretends to be the CEO or IT support.
People tend to be creatures of habit.Attacker learns your routine and strikes when you least expect it.

Memory Aid:

"Nice, Favors, Curious, Authority, Habit" — the 5 human weaknesses.

Real-World Example:

Think of social engineering like a con artist. They don't break into your house—they talk you into giving them the key.
SECTION 5: WARDRIVING

🔑 WARDRIVING

Definition: Attackers search for wireless networks with vulnerabilities while moving around an area in a moving vehicle, using hardware and software to discover unsecured Wi-Fi networks.

Key Points:

  • Attacker then gains unauthorized access by cracking passwords or decrypting the router.
  • Attacker records vulnerable network locations on digital maps — called access point mapping.
  • May share that information with third-party applications and websites.
  • Wardriving does not require special equipment — just a WLAN utility called a sniffer to detect access points and their SSIDs by intercepting beacons.

What Wardrivers Look For (Vulnerabilities):

VulnerabilityWhy It's Bad
Default usernames and passwordsEasy to guess.
Weak or nonexistent WLAN encryptionNo protection.
Default SSIDsIdentifies the router model.
Default crypto keysEasy to crack.
Default SNMP settingsCan be exploited for network management.
Default channelsPredictable.
Enabled DHCPAutomatically assigns IPs to attackers.

Memory Aid:

"Default, Weak, None, Default, Default, Default, Enabled" — the 7 deadly sins of WLAN config.

Real-World Example:

Think of wardriving like a burglar driving through a neighborhood, looking for houses with unlocked doors and windows.

🔑 WARDRIVE ATTACKS

Definition: Once a vulnerable network is found, attackers use these techniques to exploit it.

AttackDescriptionAnalogy
MasqueradingAttacker impersonates authorized users to gain their level of privileges.Wearing a fake uniform to get into a restricted area.
ReplayAttacker uses a packet analyzer to capture network traffic, then retransmits it as though from a legitimate user.Recording a password and playing it back.
Message ModificationAttacker alters, deletes, adds, or reorders the contents of a message. Attacks integrity.Changing a bank transfer amount from $10 to $10,000.
Denial of Service (DoS)Constantly transmitting on Layer 1, denying others access to the network.Blocking the door so no one can get in.

Memory Aid:

"M-R-M-D" — Masquerading, Replay, Message Modification, DoS.
SECTION 6: ROGUE ACCESS POINTS & EVIL TWINS

🔑 ROGUE ACCESS POINTS

Definition: A rogue AP is an unauthorized AP attached to the wired network. These can be installed by hackers or employees.

Key Points:

  • Leakage of RF signal from the building.
  • Offers easy access into the corporate network from a safe location.

Vulnerabilities Include:

VulnerabilityDescription
Scanning and mappingAttackers can map the WLAN network.
Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attacksIntercept and modify traffic.
Attacks on the wired networkARP poisoning, DoS attacks.
Free and unauthorized internet accessAll associated problems.
Data leakage and theftDue to unauthorized access.

🔑 RISK MITIGATION OF ROGUE APs

MitigationDescription
Network Access Control (NAC)With mutual authentication, blocks unauthorized devices from connecting.
Strict control over Ethernet portsShut down all unused ports by default. Prevents rogue APs from being inserted into live ports.

Real-World Example:

Think of NAC like a bouncer at a club. Only people on the list (authorized devices) get in. If you're not on the list, you're blocked.

🔑 EVIL TWINS

Definition: An evil twin is a rogue AP installed with sinister intent. The attacker poses as a genuine network service provider but actually eavesdrops on activities and steals information and passwords.

Key Points:

  • Attacker sets up a fake Wi-Fi access point, hoping users connect to it instead of the legitimate one.
  • When users connect, all data passes through a server controlled by the attacker.
  • Evil twin can be created by a smartphone or other internet-capable device with readily available software.
  • More common on public Wi-Fi networks (unsecured, leave personal data vulnerable).

🔑 EVIL TWIN ATTACK — WORKING (STEP BY STEP)

StepActionDescription
1Looking for the right locationCoffee shops, libraries, airports (high traffic, public Wi-Fi).
2Setting up a Wi-Fi access pointUse same SSID as legitimate network. Can be done via smartphone, tablet, portable router, or Wi-Fi Pineapple (extends range).
3Encouraging victims to connectMove closer to victims to create a stronger signal.
4Setting up a fake captive portalCopy of the public Wi-Fi login page. Victims enter credentials → attacker captures them.
5Stealing victims' dataClassic man-in-the-middle attack. Attacker monitors online activities, can insert malware.

Memory Aid:

"Location, Setup, Encourage, Portal, Steal" — the 5 steps of an evil twin attack.

🔑 EVIL TWIN — INDICATORS & SOLUTIONS

Why Evil Twins Work:

  • They look like a legitimate access point.
  • Commonly used to divert traffic to phishing websites.

Two Main Indications:

IndicatorWhy It's Suspicious
Unable to establish an HTTPS connectionLegitimate sites use HTTPS. If you can't connect securely, you may be on a fake AP.
Free internet accessBe careful and verify genuineness of an AP.

Solutions:

SolutionDescription
Port-based Network Access Control (PNAC)Robust mutual authentication.
Avoid unsecured Wi-Fi hotspotsDon't connect to unknown networks.
Disable auto-connectDon't let your device automatically join networks.
Avoid logging into private accounts on public Wi-FiUse cellular data instead.
Use multi-factor authentication (MFA)Adds an extra layer.
Stick to HTTPS websitesEncrypted connections.
Use VPN (end-to-end encryption)Protects all traffic.

Memory Aid:

"PNAC, Avoid, Disable, Avoid, MFA, HTTPS, VPN" — the 7 defenses.
SECTION 7: BLUETOOTH VULNERABILITIES & THREATS

🔑 BLUETOOTH BASICS

Definition: Bluetooth is associated with the Wireless Personal Area Network (WPAN) — it enables wearable or mobile wireless devices to peer with each other to form ad hoc wireless networks.

Key Points:

  • Connects up to 8 devices in a piconet.
  • Uses only 1 milliwatt of signal.
  • Low power usage conserves battery life but restricts range to around 10 meters.

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluetooth like a walkie-talkie with a short range. It's great for connecting your phone to your earbuds, but it doesn't reach across the building.

🔑 BLUETOOTH VERSIONS AND COMPARISON

Evolution:

  • Evolved over nearly two decades from a wireless replacement for physical RS-232 cables to ubiquitous wireless technology.
  • Success through ubiquity, flexibility, and user convenience.
  • Recent innovations focus on IoT (mesh networking, lower energy, longer range).

Notable Additions:

VersionKey Feature
2.0Enhanced data rates up to 3 Mbps (EDR).
3.0Improved high-speed data exchange over Wi-Fi (HS).
4.0Introduction of low-energy mode (LE).

Memory Aid:

"Each version: faster, farther, more efficient."

🔑 BLUETOOTH PAIRING

Definition: The Bluetooth Link Key is generated at the initialization phase when two devices on a Bluetooth channel start communicating or bonding.

Key Points:

  • Both devices derive link keys using the identical PIN entered by the user.
  • After initialization, devices perform authentication and encryption.
  • When a Bluetooth device comes near another, they begin to communicate without user initiation.
  • They check for information to share and negotiate a master/slave relationship.

Three Levels of Security:

Security LevelDescription
AuthenticationVerify the Bluetooth device address.
ConfidentialityPrevent eavesdropping.
AuthorizationEnsure a device is authorized to use a service.

Memory Aid:

"A-C-A" — Authentication, Confidentiality, Authorization.

🔑 BLUETOOTH AUTHENTICATION (CHALLENGE-RESPONSE SCHEME)

Definition: A challenge-response scheme to verify that the device requesting access knows the secret link key.

Step-by-Step:

StepAction
1Requesting device sends its unique device address to the verifying device.
2Verifying device sends a 128-bit random challenge.
3Both devices use the E1 algorithm (stream cipher) on: (i) Random challenge, (ii) Device address, (iii) Link key.
4A 32-bit result (SRES) is sent to verifier. Rest of 96 bits are Authenticated Ciphering Offset (ACO) — used for further authentication.
5Verifier compares the two results and authenticates if they match.

Key Points:

  • Bluetooth blocks repeated link key-cracking attempts by exponentially increasing the time between attempts.

Memory Aid:

"Send Address → Get Challenge → Run E1 → Send SRES → Compare."

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluetooth authentication like a secret handshake. Both people know the secret (link key), so they can verify each other without saying it out loud.

🔑 BLUETOOTH CONFIDENTIALITY

Definition: Bluetooth encrypts its data transmissions in two ways.

Encryption Methods:

MethodDescription
E0Stream cipher.
AES-CCMAdvanced Encryption Standard — Counter with CBC-MAC.

Key Stream Generation:

  • Inputs: Device address, random number, slot number, encryption key.
  • Encryption key produced from internal key generator with: Link key, random number, ACO (from authentication).

Key Point:

  • The key stream used to encrypt each data packet changes on a per-packet basis because the slot number is different each time, but all other variables remain static.

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluetooth encryption like a different lock for every message. Even if someone cracks one message, the next one uses a different key.

🔑 BLUETOOTH SECURITY MODES

Definition: Bluetooth has four security modes.

ModeApplies ToWhere Security is EnforcedDescriptionTypical Devices
Mode 1Classic (pre-2.0)No securityNo authentication or encryption at all.Very early headsets, mice, keyboards (Bluetooth 1.0/1.1 era).
Mode 2Classic (1.2, 2.0)L2CAP layer (service level)Security decisions made per service after connection setup. Some services may be protected, others not.Mid-2000s phones, PDAs, laptops.
Mode 3Classic (2.0, 2.1+)Link level (baseband)Authentication/encryption mandatory before link setup.Modern smartphones, headsets, laptops (post-2007).
Mode 4Classic (2.1 + EDR and later)Link levelAuthentication/encryption mandatory before link establishment. Mandates Secure Simple Pairing (SSP) and AES-CCM. Strength depends on association model (numeric comparison, passkey, OOB).Modern smartphones, headsets, laptops (post-2007).

Memory Aid:

"Mode 1 = None. Mode 2 = Service. Mode 3 = Link. Mode 4 = Link + SSP."

🔑 MODE 4 — FOUR SEPARATE LEVELS OF SECURITY

Definition: Mode 4 has four separate service levels.

Service LevelAuthenticationEncryptionExample Association ModelStrength
Level 0NoneNoneOpen servicesWeakest
Level 1NoYes (unauthenticated key)Just WorksWeak (no MITM protection)
Level 2Yes (legacy)YesLegacy PIN pairingMedium
Level 3YesYes (AES-CCM 128-bit)Numeric Comparison, Passkey, OOBStrongest

Memory Aid:

"Level 0 = None. Level 1 = Just Works. Level 2 = PIN. Level 3 = Strongest."

🔑 BLUEJACKING

Definition: Bluejacking came about through the misuse of a Bluetooth feature whereby a mobile phone could exchange a "business card" or messages with another phone in the vicinity.

Key Points:

  • Bluetooth devices needed to peer before messages could be communicated.
  • Initially worked because not everyone felt inconvenienced.
  • As more stores adopted this form of advertising, it became a real nuisance.

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluejacking like spam text messages sent via Bluetooth. Annoying, but not dangerous.

🔑 BLUESNARFING

Definition: Bluesnarfing is a technique whereby an attacker gains access to unauthorized information on a Bluetooth-enabled device (laptop, mobile phone).

Key Points:

  • Attacker can access contacts, calendar, emails, and text messages.
  • Bluetooth uses Frequency-Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS).
  • Exploits vulnerabilities in the Object Exchange (OBEX) protocol.
  • Active attack: Attacker tries to pair their device with the victim's without permission.
  • Passive attack (bluesniffing): Attacker only listens to the victim's Bluetooth connection and gathers data.

Key Difference:

AttackMethodGoal
BluejackingPush — sends unsolicited messages.Harmless annoyance.
BluesnarfingGet — pulls data from the device.Data theft.

What Attackers Can Do:

  • Download data from the compromised device.
  • Install malware on the device.
  • Use sensitive data to defraud other victims.
  • Commit identity theft.
  • Damage the victim's reputation.

Preventing Bluesnarfing:

  • Use a complex PIN.
  • Use two-factor authentication (2FA).
  • Approve all connection requests yourself.
  • Limit apps that have access to Bluetooth.
  • Do not accept pairing requests from unknown devices.
  • Turn off discovery mode to make it invisible to unknown devices.

Memory Aid:

"Bluejacking = Push (annoying). Bluesnarfing = Pull (stealing)."

🔑 BLUEBUGGING

Definition: Bluebugging is a hacking technique where an attacker gains unauthorized access to a victim's device via Bluetooth and takes control of it — like a "backdoor" attack for Bluetooth devices.

Step-by-Step:

StepActionDescription
1Discovery & ProfilingAttacker scans area, identifies target device (MAC address), gathers info (model, Bluetooth stack version) to find vulnerabilities.
2Establishing a Pairing ConnectionSocial engineering or technical exploitation. Attacker tricks victim into pairing (e.g., spoofs "Car-Kit 1234").
3Exploiting the Vulnerability & Installing the BackdoorOnce paired, attacker exploits a flaw, gains elevated privileges, installs hidden backdoor/malware.
4Remote Control & Data TheftAttacker controls the phone from a distance (up to 10 meters, extendable with powerful antennas).

Memory Aid:

"Discover → Pair → Exploit → Control."

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluebugging like a remote-controlled car. Once the attacker pairs with your device, they can drive it wherever they want.

🔑 BLUETOOTH ATTACK SOFTWARES

SoftwareDescription
BlueBorneVulnerability allows any affected device with Bluetooth turned on to be attacked. Flaws are in vendor software (Android, iOS, Linux, Windows).
BleedingBitDoes not require pairing. Only requires Bluetooth turned on and in range. Exploits a vulnerability in Texas Instruments Bluetooth chips.
KNOB (Key Negotiation of Bluetooth)Makes a device vulnerable to Man-in-the-Middle attack via packet injection.
SweynToothThree types of attacks: crash devices, reboot devices, force deadlock state, override security.

Memory Aid:

"BlueBorne, BleedingBit, KNOB, SweynTooth" — the 4 Bluetooth villains.

🔑 IS BLUETOOTH VULNERABLE?

Definition: In 2008, NIST published the Guide to Bluetooth Security, noting Bluetooth is susceptible to DoS attacks, eavesdropping, MITM attacks, message modification, and resource misappropriation.

Vulnerabilities Summarized:

  1. Short PINs used during pairing.
  1. Users pairing devices in public.
  1. User convenience (prioritized over security).

Real-World Example:

Think of Bluetooth security like a cheap lock. It keeps honest people out, but a determined thief can pick it.
SECTION 8: PACKET ANALYSIS

🔑 PACKET ANALYSIS

Definition: The practice of capturing and deciphering packets being transmitted across the air interface.

Key Points:

  • Most popular tool: Airshark (wireless version of Wireshark).
  • On a wired network: To see all traffic, a network administrator must enable port mirroring — all traffic on specified ports is replicated on the mirroring port.
  • On a wireless network: All traffic is visible on the same frequencies and channels, making it easily intercepted and captured.

Real-World Example:

Think of packet analysis like reading someone's mail. On a wired network, you need special permission (port mirroring). On a wireless network, the mail is just flying through the air, and anyone with a net can catch it.

🔑 WIRELESS NETWORKS AND INFORMATION THEFT

Definition: Wireless networks are susceptible to information theft because they transmit to anyone who cares to listen.

Key Points:

  • All that is necessary is an antenna and a receiver tuned to the correct frequency.
  • Common consensus suggested wireless could be used only at the perimeter of the network and behind the firewall.
  • With web services and mobile devices, network designers had to cater to wireless.

Real-World Example:

Think of wireless like a radio broadcast. Anyone with a radio can listen in. You can't stop the signal from leaving your building.

🔑 MALICIOUS DATA INSERTION ON WIRELESS NETWORKS

Definition: Data thieves do not remove information — they simply copy it. The original data is left intact.

Key Points:

  • Malicious data injection: Modify data in some way for their own gain.
  • If the network does not use encryption, inserting malicious data is a simple task.
  • To make the task harder, encrypt the network.

Real-World Example:

Think of malicious data insertion like changing a price tag in a store. The item is still there, but the price is now wrong.
SECTION 9: DENIAL OF SERVICE ATTACKS

🔑 DENIAL OF SERVICE (DoS) ATTACKS

Definition: Wireless networks are particularly vulnerable to DoS attacks because they operate on a half-duplex collision-detection medium. Only one radio client station can talk at a time, including the access point.

Key Points:

  • Devices listen for traffic before transmitting. If something else is transmitting, they wait.
  • DoS attacks on wireless can be categorized into several groups.

DoS Attack Categories:

LayerAttack TypeDescription
Application LayerOverwhelm the application server.Prevent it from handling requests (e.g., HTTP GET requests).
Transport LayerConsume finite TCP sockets.E.g., TCP SYN flood attack.
Network LayerFlood bandwidth capacity.Send large amounts of data (e.g., ICMP request).
MAC LayerSend authenticate/associate request flood.Overwhelm the AP with connection requests.
Physical LayerCause interference using high-gain antennas.Create noise to disrupt signal.

Memory Aid:

"A-T-N-M-P" — Application, Transport, Network, MAC, Physical.

Real-World Example:

Think of a DoS attack like a crowd of people standing in a doorway. No one can get in or out because the space is filled.
SECTION 10: PEER-TO-PEER HACKING & UNAUTHORIZED CONTROL

🔑 PEER-TO-PEER HACKING OVER AD HOC NETWORKS

Definition: Wireless networks configured using the standard take two forms.

Network TypeDescription
Infrastructure NetworksRequire a central access point to serve as the hub. Client stations communicate via the AP.
Ad Hoc NetworksPeer-to-peer relationships formed directly between client stations. Informal networks.

Ad Hoc Network Issues:

  • Frequently cause interference and degradation of service to both ad hoc and corporate networks.
  • No way of authenticating clients — any device configured in ad hoc mode can connect to any other ad hoc station.

Real-World Example:

Think of ad hoc networks like a group of strangers forming a circle and talking. Anyone can join, and no one checks IDs.

🔑 WHEN AN ATTACKER GAINS UNAUTHORIZED CONTROL

Definition: With ad hoc networks, it is possible for an attacker to gain unauthorized access and control because device authentication is not robust.

Security Measures:

MeasureDescription
WEP, WPA, or WPA2Secure access points from unauthorized authentication and association. Use the most robust (WPA2).
Network CloakingDo not advertise the SSID in the beacon. Prevents casual attacks.
Downside of CloakingCauses client devices to probe for it (active scanning), which can reveal the SSID anyway.

Real-World Example:

Think of network cloaking like hiding your house number. It stops casual passersby, but if someone really wants to find you, they can still look for clues.

✅ THE CORE TRUTH
Wireless networks are inherently vulnerable because they broadcast signals into the air where anyone can listen. Attackers range from unskilled opportunists to highly skilled insiders. Social engineering exploits human nature, wardriving finds unsecured networks, rogue APs and evil twins compromise traffic, and Bluetooth has its own family of attacks. The best defenses are layered: strong encryption (WPA2/WPA3), physical security, user awareness, and shutting down unused ports.
