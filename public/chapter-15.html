CHAPTER 15: MOBILE MALWARE AND APPLICATION BASED THREATS

SECTION 1: MALWARE ON ANDROID vs. iOS

🔑 MALWARE ON ANDROID DEVICES

Definition: Android is a frequent target due to its large user base, diverse ecosystem, and open distribution.

Key Points:

  • Why Android? Large installed base, diverse devices, opportunities for malicious app distribution.
  • Openness ≠ Insecure: Security depends on architecture, patching, and user behavior.
  • Core Protections: App sandboxing, verified boot, SELinux, permissions, Google Play Protect.

Software Fragmentation:

  • Android devices vary wildly (manufacturer, chipset, OS version).
  • The Problem: Diversity delays security patches, leaving some devices exposed.
  • Important: Patch delays do not mean every Android device is vulnerable. Malware counts depend on the dataset, year, and method, so they should not be treated as overall infection rates.
  • Risk Reduction: Supported devices, timely updates, device inventory, patch-compliance policies.
  • Sideloading: Increases risk if apps come from untrusted sources (but not inherently malicious).

Real-World Example:

Think of fragmentation like a fleet of cars from 20 different manufacturers. When a recall (security patch) happens, getting the fix to all 20 models takes a long time.

📊 MALWARE ON APPLE iOS DEVICES

Definition: iOS uses strict controls, but is not immune.

Key Points:

  • Protections: Code signing, sandboxing, permission controls, restricted app distribution.
  • Vulnerabilities: Phishing, malicious profiles, software exploits, targeted spyware.
  • Reality Check: Updates reduce risk but do not guarantee immunity (especially against zero-days).
  • Compare carefully: Consider threat model, device support, patch level, and deployment context instead of claiming one platform is absolutely safe.

SECTION 2: CYBERCRIMINAL GOALS & MALWARE CATEGORIES

🔑 CRIMINAL AND DEVELOPER COLLABORATION (MALWARE CATEGORIES)

Definition: Cybercriminals use specific types of malware to achieve their goals.

CategoryDescription
RATs (Remote Access Tools)Backdoor access to infected devices for intelligence collection.
Bank TrojansSteals login credentials for mobile banking apps.
RansomwareLocks user out by encrypting data.
Cryptomining MalwareCovertly mines cryptocurrency on the victim's device.
Advertising Click FraudInfiltrates devices to generate income via fake ad clicks.
Stalkerware (Trackers)Steals GPS coordinates and intercepts text messages.
Stalkerware (Stalkers)Steals any data (photos, texts, contacts, screen taps).

The 5 Goals of Cybercriminals:

  1. Data Theft: Steal personal/confidential info from the phone.
  1. Botnet/DDoS: Control phones to launch attacks or send premium SMS.
  1. Tracking: Steal GPS/location data to sell to advertisers.
  1. Ransom: Steal data/files or lock the device.
  1. Surveillance: Control the camera and microphone.

The Inherent Risks They Exploit:

  • Insecure data storage
  • Weak server-side controls
  • Insufficient transport layer protection
  • Poor authorization/authentication
  • Improper session handling
  • Data leakage
  • Poor encryption/Sensitive data disclosure

Memory Aid:

"RATs, Banks, Ransom, Crypto, Clicks, Stalkers."

🔑 MADWARE (MOBILE ADWARE)

Definition: A very aggressive form of adware prevalent on mobile devices.

Key Points:

  • The "Free App" Business Model: Developers consider madware legitimate because it funds free apps.
  • Benign vs. Malicious:
    • Some are benign.
    • Others run background processes accessing GPS, scanning address books, and sending stolen data via HTTP. Some also share location details or send collected data through third-party APIs.
  • The Excuse: Developers claim the terms of use were accepted (even if users didn't read them).

Real-World Example:

Think of madware like spam mail that also opens your drawers and reads your diary while you're looking at the junk advertisements.

⚠️ EXCESSIVE APPLICATION PERMISSION

Definition: Apps requesting permissions they don't need.

Case Study: The Flashlight App

  • Legitimate: Needs camera/flashlight access (depending on OS).
  • Warning Signs: Requests location, contacts, microphone, and accessibility.
  • Check the context: Ask why a permission is needed, when it is requested, and what data is collected. Distinguish normal, runtime, and special/privileged permissions.
  • Other warning signs include a suspicious developer identity or unexplained background behavior. Malicious apps may also use staged downloads, accessibility abuse, or deceptive updates.
  • Reality: On many modern Android devices, a basic flashlight does not need broad personal-data permissions; implementation and OS version matter.

Recommended Controls:

  • Least privilege.
  • Play Protect.
  • OS updates.
  • Remove unneeded apps.

Memory Aid:

"If a flashlight asks for your contacts, it's a spy, not a light."
SECTION 3: DELIVERY METHODS & SOCIAL ENGINEERING

🔑 MOBILE MALWARE DELIVERY METHODS

Definition: How cybercriminals get the malware onto the device.

The Affiliate Program Model:

  • Cybercriminals pay people a commission to deliver the payload (like legitimate affiliate marketing).

The 7 Delivery Tactics:

  1. Binding: Attaching malware to a genuine, popular free app.
  1. 3rd Party Stores: Loading malware to unofficial app stores.
  1. Drive-by Download: Creating an infected webpage.
  1. Hybrid Attack: Dual payload targeting PCs and smartphones.
  1. Social Engineering: Tricking the user.
  1. Malicious QR Codes: (Quishing).
  1. Trojan Dropper: A program that looks harmless but installs malware.

🔑 MOBILE MALWARE & SOCIAL ENGINEERING

Definition: Manipulating people into installing apps, disclosing credentials, or granting permissions.

Common Lures:

  • SMS phishing (Smishing)
  • QR-code phishing (Quishing)
  • Fake delivery alerts
  • Fake updates
  • Impersonated support

Defenses:

  • Training, verified app distribution, phishing-resistant authentication, managed-device controls.
  • Key Concept: Users are not the only weak link; design and configuration matter too.

⚠️ CAPTIVE PORTALS

Definition: A web page used by a Wi-Fi network for access, terms, or authentication.

Key Points:

  • Not inherently malicious: It's a standard tool.
  • The Exploit: Attackers imitate a legitimate portal to collect credentials or redirect to phishing pages.
  • Clarification: A portal alone does not automatically install a Trojan. The user must still download something or fall for an exploit.

Defenses: Verify the network, avoid entering unrelated credentials, use HTTPS.

SECTION 4: BROWSER & WEB ATTACKS

🔑 DRIVE-BY ATTACKS

Definition: A website triggers exploitation of a vulnerable browser or component.

Key Points:

  • Visiting a malicious site does not guarantee infection (depends on vulnerabilities and security controls).
  • Compromised legitimate websites can also serve malicious content.
  • Defenses: Prompt updates, phishing/malware protection, avoid untrusted downloads.

🔑 CLICKJACKING & LIKEJACKING

Definition: Deceptive interface layering causes a user to click a different control than intended.

TypeDescription
ClickjackingDisguised consent button, misleading overlay, or embedded interaction.
LikejackingA social-media variant where an invisible frame may trick a user into activating another control. The result depends on the action triggered and browser defenses; it does not automatically install malware.
  • Why Likejacking Works: Small smartphone screens make it hard to see the invisible overlay.
  • Defenses: Interface safeguards, user awareness. Neither automatically installs malware (depends on the action induced).

🔑 PLUG-AND-PLAY SCRIPTS

Definition: Browser scripts (JavaScript) abused in malicious pages.

Key Points:

  • A tap or click alone does not install malware (requires a vulnerability, unsafe download, or permission grant).
  • Outdated Guidance: Blanket disabling of JavaScript is outdated and not recommended for modern mobile browsers.
  • Modern Defense: Prefer up-to-date browsers, safe browsing protections, careful download review, and minimal permissions.

🔑 MITIGATING MOBILE BROWSER ATTACKS

The Checklist:

  1. Keep OS, browser, and apps updated.
  1. Use HTTPS and verify destinations (HTTPS ≠ trustworthy site).
  1. Enable phishing/malware warnings.
  1. Install apps from trusted sources.
  1. Use device security/strong authentication.
  1. Remove unused apps.
  1. Limit autofill on shared devices.
  1. Block abusive pop-ups/Safe Browsing.
  1. Clear site data for privacy (but note: deleting history does not remove malware).
  1. Avoid blanket disabling of JavaScript/HTML5.
SECTION 5: MOBILE APPLICATION ATTACKS & DEFENSE

🔑 THE TWO MAIN CATEGORIES OF APP EXPLOITS

Definition: Mobile app security exploits result from one of two main causes.

CauseDescriptionExamples
1. Malicious Unnecessary FunctionalityUnwanted & dangerous code hidden in an app. Users think they are downloading a game, but it's loaded with spyware, adware, or premium SMS links.Fake games, fake utility apps.
2. Errors in Design and ImplementationComplexity of code, coded by amateurs, not designed with security in mind.Sensitive data leakage, unsafe storage, unsecure communication, hard-coded passwords/keys.

The Monetization Problem:

  • Many apps are free. Developers monetize by selling users' location and browsing history to ad networks.

🔑 MOBILE MALWARE DEFENSE

Definition: Mobile security is layered; no single antivirus detects everything.

PlatformDefenses
AndroidPlay Protect, app sandboxing, SELinux, verified boot, timely patching.
iOSCode signing, sandboxing, permission controls, timely updates.
EnterpriseVetted apps, least privilege, compliance monitoring, phishing-resistant auth, incident response.

Limitation: Security tools may have limited visibility due to platform sandboxing. Evaluate each tool's actual capabilities.

🔑 MOBILE DEVICE MANAGEMENT (MDM) & BYOD

Definition: MDM enforces configuration, tracks compliance, and distributes approved apps.

Key Points:

  • BYOD Policies: Define permitted devices, minimum OS versions, encryption, screen lock.
  • Work Profiles: Android work profiles and managed iOS enrollment separate organizational and personal info.
  • Selective removal: MDM can support removing corporate data separately from personal data.
  • Conditional Access: Block noncompliant devices from corporate resources.
  • Remote Wipe: Must follow policy, privacy requirements, and employee consent/notice.

Real-World Example:

Think of MDM like a company security guard checking IDs (compliance) at the door. If you don't meet the dress code (OS version, encryption), you can't enter the corporate party.

✅ THE CORE TRUTH
Mobile malware succeeds through a combination of user deception (social engineering, excessive permissions) and technical flaws (fragmentation, insecure design). Android is a bigger target due to its openness, but iOS is also vulnerable, especially when jailbroken. Defense requires a layered approach: user awareness, strict permissions, up-to-date OS/apps, and enterprise MDM policies.
