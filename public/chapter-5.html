CHAPTER 5: HOW DO WLANs WORK?

SECTION 1: WLAN TOPOLOGIES & ISM SPECTRUM

🔑 WLAN TOPOLOGIES (THE FUNDAMENTAL RULE)

Definition: The fundamental requirement of any wireless communication is that devices communicate over a common radio frequency. Each device must be tuned to a specific frequency within the ISM unlicensed spectrum.

Key Points:

  • All devices in a WLAN must be tuned to the same radio frequency to communicate.
  • WLAN operates within the ISM Unlicensed Spectrum.

Real-World Example:

Think of a WLAN like a group of people on walkie-talkies. Everyone must be on the same channel to hear each other. If you're on Channel 3 and your friend is on Channel 7, you can't talk.

🔑 ISM UNLICENSED SPECTRUM

Definition: ISM stands for Industrial, Scientific, and Medical band of unlicensed wireless spectrum.

Key Points:

  • Available for short-range wireless communication:
    • PAN (Personal Area Network)
    • WLAN (Wireless Local Area Network)
    • WWAN (Wireless Wide Area Network)
  • Objective: Provide easier access to wireless spectrum for innovators and product developers.
  • Government approval required: Developers need prior approval before product deployment (comply with local and ITU international policies).

Real-World Example:

Think of the ISM band like a public park. Anyone can use it (no license needed), but there are rules (power limits, certified equipment) to keep everyone from interfering with each other.

SECTION 2: WLAN ANATOMY & CLIENT DEVICES

🔑 WLAN ANATOMY (THE BUILDING BLOCKS)

Definition: The main component of any WLAN is a radio card, referred to as a station (STA).

Key Points:

  • STA (Station): Any 802.11-capable device (laptop, smartphone, etc.).
  • Client Station: An endpoint device (the one you use).
  • Access Point (AP): Also a STA. Serves as the central hub that communicates with all client stations.
  • Wired Connection: AP is typically wired to a fixed-line switch (gateway to Ethernet network).
  • Bridge Mode: An AP can also act as a bridge, connecting to another AP.

Real-World Example:

Think of an AP like a cell tower. Your phone (client station) talks to the tower (AP), and the tower connects to the main phone network (Ethernet).

🔑 WIRELESS CLIENT DEVICES (THE 3 CRITERIA)

Definition: Any device that meets these three criteria can act as a wireless client:

  1. Contains a radio card or integrated transmitter and receiver.
  1. Contains an antenna.
  1. Operates under 802.11 protocol standards.

Key Points:

  • Every AP is identified by a Service Set Identifier (SSID) — a configurable name or alphanumeric code.
  • Before a client can connect, it must detect the AP's presence.

Two Scanning Methods:

Scanning TypeHow It WorksAnalogy
Passive ScanningClient listens for a beacon that the AP continually emits. If it "hears" a beacon advertising an SSID it's been configured for, it selects that AP.Waiting for someone to call your name.
Active ScanningClient proactively sends out probe pulse requests.Walking around asking "Is anyone there?"

Real-World Example:

Think of passive scanning like waiting for a radio station to announce its name.

Think of active scanning like spinning the dial and stopping when you hear a station you like.

Definition: A client station can operate in one of two configurable modes.

Real-World Example:

Think of infrastructure mode like a phone call through a cell tower (everything goes through the tower).

Think of ad hoc mode like walkie-talkies (direct device-to-device).

SECTION 5: UNLICENSED BANDS & FREQUENCY SPREADING

🔑 802.11 UNLICENSED BANDS (FCC RULES)

Definition: The use of radio waves falls under the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) , which controls usage through licensing.

Key Points:

  • Licensed use: Government or commercial entity uses a designated frequency without fear of interference.
  • Unlicensed bands: Users can operate without an FCC license, but:
    • Must use certified radio equipment.
    • Must comply with technical requirements (power limits).
    • No exclusive use — subject to interference.

Real-World Example:

Think of licensed frequencies like a private road (only you can drive on it).

Unlicensed bands are like a public highway (anyone can drive, but there's traffic and you might get stuck).

🔑 NARROWBAND vs SPREAD SPECTRUM

Definition: Two techniques for radio transmission over a given spectrum.

TechniqueHow It WorksPros/ConsAnalogy
NarrowbandUses very little bandwidth by transmitting over a narrow beam of frequency.Con: Susceptible to frequency jamming (tight frequency bands).A laser pointer — precise but easily blocked.
Spread SpectrumTransmission is spread across the entire frequency space.Pro: Much harder to block (wide frequency band).A floodlight — covers everything, hard to block.

Also Note: Voiceband (300–3400 Hz) is also a narrowband.

🔑 FREQUENCY HOPPING SPREAD SPECTRUM (FHSS)

Definition: FHSS works by transmitting a small carrier space in short bursts and then continuously changing ("hopping") to another frequency during transmission.

How FHSS Works (Diagram):

Original Signal → Modulator → Spread Signal
                     ↑
            Frequency Synthesizer
                     ↑
         Pseudorandom Code Generator → Frequency Table

The Hopping Pattern:

  • The signal hops between frequencies in a pseudorandom sequence.
  • Cycle 1: Hops between frequencies (e.g., 900 → 700 → 300 → 200 → 400 → 800 → 500 → 600).
  • Cycle 2: Hops in a different pattern (e.g., 700 → 900 → 300 → 200 → 400 → 800 → 500 → 600).

Memory Aid:

"FHSS = Frequency Hopping = Jumping around the radio dial."

Real-World Example:

Think of FHSS like a spy changing radio channels every few seconds so the enemy can't listen in. Even if one channel is jammed, the message continues on another.

🔑 DIRECT SEQUENCE SPREAD SPECTRUM (DSSS)

Definition: DSSS uses hopping. However, unlike FHSS which hops between frequencies, DSSS stays on a fixed channel, hopping within the frequency space on that channel using a technique called data encoding.

How DSSS Works (Diagram):

Original Signal → Modulator → Spread Signal
                     ↑
              Chips Generator

The Spread Signal Concept:

  • Each data bit is multiplied by a spreading code (a sequence of chips).
  • Example: Original signal = 1, 0, 1 → Spreading code multiplies each bit into multiple chips → Spread signal.

Memory Aid:

"DSSS = Direct Sequence = One channel, spreading within it."

Real-World Example:

Think of DSSS like a crowd singing the same song. Even if some people are off-key (interference), the overall song is still recognizable. The more people singing (chips), the clearer the song.
SECTION 6: MULTIPATH FADING & INTERFERENCE

🔑 MULTIPATH FADING

Definition: Radio waves reflect off certain materials and surfaces, resulting in multiple versions of the same radio wave bouncing around — known as multipath.

Key Points:

  • Direct wave: Travels straight from transmitter to receiver.
  • Reflected wave: Bounces off buildings, walls, ground.
  • Diffracted wave: Bends around obstacles.
  • Scattered wave: Redirected by small objects (trees, vehicles, signs).
  • Resultant wave: The combination of all these waves.

🔑 MULTIPATH PROPAGATION: WHY MULTIPLE SIGNALS ARRIVE

Definition: Multipath propagation occurs when a radio signal reaches the receiver through multiple paths rather than only one direct path.

The 4 Path Types:

Path TypeDescriptionAnalogy
Direct (LOS)Signal travels directly from transmitter to receiver.A straight line from A to B.
ReflectionSignal bounces from large surfaces (buildings, walls, ground).A billiard ball bouncing off a wall.
DiffractionSignal bends around the edges of obstacles (buildings, hills).Water flowing around a rock.
ScatteringSignal is redirected by smaller or irregular objects (trees, vehicles, street signs).Light scattering through fog.

Key Point: Different path lengths produce different delays, amplitudes, and phases.

Real-World Example:

Think of a smartphone on Wi-Fi receiving the same signal directly and as copies reflected from walls, furniture, and the floor. Your phone hears an echo of the same signal.

🔑 MULTIPATH FADING: CONSTRUCTIVE & DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE

Definition: At the receiver, different copies of the signal combine with one another.

TypeWhat HappensResultAnalogy
Constructive InterferenceSignals arrive approximately in phase → reinforce each other.Stronger signalTwo people pushing a swing at the same time.
Destructive InterferenceSignals arrive out of phase → partially or completely cancel.Weaker signal or fadingTwo people pushing a swing at opposite times.

Key Points:

  • Small changes in receiver position alter path lengths and phases → received signal strength can vary rapidly.
  • Inter-Symbol Interference (ISI): Delayed copies may overlap later symbols.

Moving Vehicle Example:

As a car moves, direct and reflected path lengths continuously change. The received signal can vary from strong → weak → strong even when transmitter power is constant.

Techniques to Reduce Fading:

TechniqueWhat It Does
OFDMOrthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing — splits signal across many subcarriers.
MIMOMultiple Input Multiple Output — multiple antennas.
DiversityUses multiple antennas to pick the best signal.
EqualizationAdjusts signal to compensate for distortion.
Adaptive ModulationChanges modulation based on conditions.

Memory Aid:

"O-M-D-E-A" → OFDM, MIMO, Diversity, Equalization, Adaptive Modulation.
SECTION 7: WIRELESS ACCESS POINTS (WAPs)

🔑 WIRELESS ACCESS POINTS (WAPs)

Definition: A WAP is a half-duplex switch that contains a radio card and an antenna tunable to one or more unlicensed radio frequencies (2.4 GHz and/or 5 GHz).

Key Points:

  • Different WAPs can operate on several channels within different frequencies.
  • Unlicensed channels are heavily populated (especially 2.4 GHz).
  • Common misconception: Tuning multiple WAPs to the same frequency/channel (e.g., 2.4 GHz Channel 6) will boost the signal.
  • Reality: If neighboring networks share the same frequency/channel, it attenuates the signal significantly.
  • Solution: Network designers configure WAPs to use non-overlapping channel plans to avoid interference.

DSSS Channel Width = 22 MHz

Non-Overlapping Channels (2.4 GHz):

Channel 1 (2412 MHz) | Channel 6 (2437 MHz) | Channel 11 (2462 MHz) | Channel 14 (2484 MHz)

Memory Aid:

"1, 6, 11 — the non-overlapping channels."

Real-World Example:

Think of overlapping channels like two radio stations on the same frequency. You hear both, but neither clearly. Network designers space them out (1, 6, 11) so each channel has its own "space."

🔑 WORKING OF A WAP

Definition: A WAP acts as a portal for another network, typically on another physical medium such as wired Ethernet.

Key Points:

  • Connects wireless clients over the air.
  • Redirects traffic to another wireless client or a wired network.
  • Uses RF signals as a carrier band frequency.
  • Frequencies assigned to 802.11: unlicensed bands at 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz.
  • WAP radio cards are configured to work on one of two unlicensed bands and on several channels.
  • WAP continuously transmits beacons on the selected RF band and channel to advertise its presence.
  • Client stations listen for WAP beacons through passive or active scanning.

Real-World Example:

Think of a WAP like a receptionist. It greets you (beacon), asks who you are (authentication), and connects you to the right department (network).
SECTION 8: WEP, WPA, WPA2 PROTOCOLS

🔑 WEP PROTOCOL – MESSAGE FLOW

Definition: Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) uses a pre-shared secret key and RC4. The sender encrypts data using an IV and ICV; the receiver uses the transmitted IV and the same shared key to regenerate the keystream and recover the data.

Notation Table:

NotationExplanation
IV24-bit Initialization Vector
KShared WEP secret key
ICVCRC-32 integrity value
RC4Stream cipher
CCiphertext

Message Flow (Diagram):

Sender / STA                                          Receiver / AP
      |                                                      |
      | 1. Compute ICV = CRC-32(P)                           |
      |                                                      |
      | 2. RC4(IV || K) XOR (P || ICV)                       |
      |                                                      |
      | 3. Sender / STA → Receiver / AP: IV || Ciphertext    |
      |----------------------------------------------------->|
      |                                                      |
      |                              4. Decrypt + verify ICV |

Key Point: The WEP data frame travels from the station to the access point.

Memory Aid:

"WEP = Weak Encryption Protocol."

Real-World Example:

Think of WEP like a cheap lock on a diary. It looks like it's protecting your secrets, but anyone with a paperclip can open it.

🔑 WPA PROTOCOL – FOUR-WAY HANDSHAKE

Definition: Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA) was introduced as an interim replacement for WEP. It retains RC4 but adds TKIP, per-packet key mixing, a 48-bit sequence counter, and MIC to provide substantially stronger confidentiality and integrity than WEP.

Notation Table:

NotationExplanation
PMKPairwise Master Key
PTKPairwise Transient Key
ANonceNonce generated by AP
SNonceNonce generated by STA
GTKGroup Temporal Key
MICMessage Integrity Code

Four-Way Handshake (Diagram):

Client / STA                                    Authenticator / AP
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 1: AP → STA {ANonce}                   |
      |<-------------------------------------------------|
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 2: STA → AP {SNonce + MIC}             |
      |------------------------------------------------->|
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 3: AP → STA {Install PTK + GTK + MIC}  |
      |<-------------------------------------------------|
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 4: STA → AP {ACK + MIC}                |
      |------------------------------------------------->|
      |                                                  |
After Message 4: Both sides have installed the PTK; the STA also has the GTK for broadcast/multicast traffic.

Memory Aid:

"ANonce → SNonce → PTK/GTK → ACK."

Real-World Example:

Think of the four-way handshake like a secret handshake between friends:
  1. AP: "Here's my secret (ANonce)."
  1. STA: "Here's mine (SNonce) + proof it's me (MIC)."
  1. AP: "Here's the group key (GTK) + proof."
  1. STA: "Confirmed (ACK)."

🔑 WPA2 PROTOCOL – FOUR-WAY HANDSHAKE

Definition: Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (WPA2) uses a four-way handshake for association. This process provides a mechanism for association and produces an encryption key used to read data.

Notation Table:

NotationExplanation
PMKPairwise Master Key
PTKPairwise Transient Key
GTKGroup Temporal Key
GMKGroup Master Key
ANonceNonce by AP
SNonceNonce by STA
MICMessage Integrity Code
EAPOLExtensible Authentication Protocol over LAN

Four-Way Handshake (Diagram):

Client-STA                                          Authenticator-AP
      |                                                  |
      |   a) PMK is known                                |
      |   b) Generate SNonce                             |
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 1: EAPOL-Key (ANonce, Unicast)         |
      |<-------------------------------------------------|
      |                                                  |
      |   Derive PTK                                     |
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 2: EAPOL-Key (SNonce, Unicast, MIC)    |
      |------------------------------------------------->|
      |                                                  |
      |                     Derive PTK                   |
      |                     If needed, generate GTK      |
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 3: EAPOL-Key (Install PTK, Unicast,    |
      |              MIC, Encrypted GTK)                 |
      |<-------------------------------------------------|
      |                                                  |
      |   Message 4: EAPOL-Key (Unicast, MIC)            |
      |------------------------------------------------->|
      |                                                  |
Install PTK and GTK                                  Install PTK
      |                                                  |
      |   IEEE 802.1X controlled port unblocked          |
      |                                                  |

Step-by-Step Explanation:

  1. WAP sends ANonce. Client uses this to create PTK.
    • PTK = PMK + ANonce + SNonce + MAC(AA) + MAC(SA)
    • MAC(AA) = Physical address of AP
    • MAC(SA) = Physical address of STA-client
  1. Client sends SNonce + MIC. WAP uses SNonce to create the key needed to decrypt messages from the client.
  1. WAP replies with GTK + MIC. GTK is used to decrypt broadcast messages.
  1. Client sends confirmation. Completes the association.

After Association:

  • Client sends a DHCP request to obtain an IP address and DNS server settings.

Memory Aid:

"ANonce → SNonce → GTK → ACK."

"PTK = PMK + ANonce + SNonce + MAC(AA) + MAC(SA)."

SECTION 10: WIRELESS BRIDGES & GATEWAYS

🔑 WIRELESS BRIDGES

Definition: The 802.11 standard describes a mechanism called a Wireless Distribution System (WDS) used to connect WAPs and form a wireless backbone.

Key Points:

  • A wireless bridge employs:
    • One radio device operating on 2.4 GHz to communicate with WLAN clients.
    • Another radio device configured to operate on 5 GHz to serve the backhaul.
  • 802.11 bridges are not ideal for backhaul but can serve a purpose when no Ethernet backhaul is available.

Real-World Example:

Think of a wireless bridge like a rope bridge between two buildings. It's not as good as a concrete bridge (Ethernet), but it gets the job done when there's no other option.

🔑 WIRELESS WORKGROUP BRIDGES

Definition: A wireless workgroup bridge is often used when there are several non-wireless devices (like Ethernet networked PCs) that require backup wireless connectivity.

Key Points:

  • Handy when non-wireless equipment needs to connect to a wireless domain.
  • The workgroup bridge is a wireless client — it can communicate only with the WAP, not another client.
  • Wired Ethernet connection on the workgroup bridge is typically half-duplex — has limitations.

Real-World Example:

Think of a workgroup bridge like a translator for a group of people who only speak Ethernet. The translator (bridge) talks to the Wi-Fi world on their behalf.

🔑 RESIDENTIAL GATEWAYS

Definition: A wireless residential gateway is a home wireless router that acts as a gateway to the internet through a DSL or cable broadband connection.

Key Points:

  • Simply two devices in one: an 802.11 WAP and an Ethernet switch.
  • Some ISPs offer a single device that serves as:
    • WLAN router
    • Ethernet switch
    • Wireless WAP
  • Job: Provide internet connectivity to various devices via wired and wireless LAN.

Configuration Options:

ConfigurationExamples

🔑 ENTERPRISE GATEWAYS

Definition: The difference between a residential and an enterprise wireless gateway is simply a matter of capabilities to handle large numbers of connections and scale.

Key Points:

  • Residential gateway: may struggle to support more than 10 simultaneous connections.
  • Enterprise gateway: expected to support more than 100 devices.
  • Typically has a WLAN and LAN interface — acts as a translational bridge between the two mediums.
  • Typically deployed as a guest point of access to the internet, with no direct access to the corporate network.

Real-World Example:

Think of a residential gateway like a family car (fits 5 people). An enterprise gateway is like a bus (fits 100+ people).
SECTION 11: WIRELESS ANTENNAS

🔑 THE THREE MAIN CATEGORIES OF ANTENNAS

Definition: Antennas are central to the performance of any wireless device.

CategoryCoverageUse CaseAnalogy
Omnidirectional360 degrees horizontally, 7–80 degrees vertically.General purpose; installed by default on WAPs.A light bulb — radiates in all directions.
Semi-DirectionalLimited to ~180 degrees.Coverage required in a specific direction.A flashlight — focused but still broad.
Highly DirectionalVery narrow beam.Point-to-point situations; very exact, high-gain beam.A laser pointer — very focused, long distance.

Memory Aid:

"Omni = All, Semi = Some, Highly = One."

🔑 OMNIDIRECTIONAL ANTENNAS

Definition: Radio waves are transmitted in both horizontal and vertical patterns.

Key Points:

  • Radiates 360 degrees on the horizontal plane.
  • Radiates between 7 and 80 degrees on the vertical plane.
  • Typically used in point-to-multipoint configurations.
  • Range can easily extend beyond the physical limits of a building.
  • Security risk: A hacker could sit in an adjacent building and probe for weaknesses.

Real-World Example:

Think of an omnidirectional antenna like a streetlamp. It lights up everything around it in a circle, but it also lights up the street outside your property (security risk).

🔑 SEMI-DIRECTIONAL ANTENNAS

Definition: Semi-directional antennas restrict unnecessary radiation, directing the focus in one direction.

Key Points:

  • Used in short- to middle-range point-to-point configurations (e.g., connecting campus buildings).
  • Most common types:
    • Yagi antennas: Range up to two miles; used in outdoor point-to-point configurations.
    • Planar antennas: Flat, panel-style antennas.

Real-World Example:

Think of a semi-directional antenna like a spotlight on a stage. It focuses light on the performer (one direction) rather than lighting up the whole theater.

🔑 HIGHLY DIRECTIONAL ANTENNAS

Definition: Highly directional antennas are tightly focused with high gain and a narrow beam.

Key Points:

  • Used in point-to-point connections.
  • Suitable for long distances, up to 35 miles.
  • Two types:
    • Grid antenna: Uses a wire grill. Spacing between wires determined by frequency.
    • Parabolic antenna: Resembles a small satellite dish.

Real-World Example:

Think of a highly directional antenna like a laser beam. It goes very far in one direction, but if you're not in the beam's path, you get nothing.

🔑 MIMO ANTENNAS

Definition: Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) antennas are a newer technology that allows multiple antennas to transmit and receive concurrently.

Key Points:

  • Complex signal-processing techniques provide:
    • Enhanced range
    • Enhanced throughput
    • Enhanced reliability
  • Uses multiple radio frequencies and channels.
  • Improves security.

Real-World Example:

Think of MIMO like a highway with multiple lanes. More lanes (antennas) = more cars (data) can travel at once, and if one lane is blocked, others keep moving.

SECTION 12: SITE SURVEYS

🔑 SITE SURVEYS

Definition: A site survey determines how RF radiation will behave within a facility, taking into account coverage, interference, and gaps based on antenna placement and power considerations.

Key Components:

  • Spectrum and protocol analysis
  • Source of noise and interference
  • Coverage analysis
  • Access point placement
  • Coverage assessment
  • Self-organizing WLANs

🔑 SPECTRUM AND PROTOCOL ANALYSIS

Definition: The first step is to perform a spectrum and protocol analysis to ensure neighboring devices do not cause interference.

Key Points:

  • Spectrum analyzer: A test device that measures the amplitude and frequency of electromagnetic signals.
  • Enables you to visualize and map existing RF activity within the 2.4 and 5.0 GHz spectrums.

Real-World Example:

Think of a spectrum analyzer like a radio scanner. It tells you what stations (signals) are already broadcasting in your area before you set up your own.

🔑 COVERAGE ANALYSIS

Definition: The first step in coverage analysis is to conduct site coverage and capacity interviews.

Key Points:

  • Establish capacity and coverage requirements based on population density.
  • May determine the need for smaller or larger cells, or even collocation.
  • Interviews should consider:
    • Density of client stations
    • Applications used

Real-World Example:

Think of coverage analysis like planning a party. You need to know how many people are coming (density) and what they'll be doing (applications) to decide how many tables and chairs (APs) you need.

🔑 ACCESS POINT PLACEMENT AND COVERAGE ASSESSMENT

Access Point Placement:

  • Location and boundaries of access points.
  • A mixture of omnidirectional and semi-directional antennas should be used to generate RF coverage that fulfills requirements while restricting unnecessary propagation.

Coverage Assessment:

ModeHow It WorksAdvantage
Passive ModeA client card collects RF measurements: receiving signal levels, signal noise, signal-to-noise ratios.Simple, no connection needed.
Active ModeClient station authenticates and associates with various access points.Upper-layer performance measurements available: packet loss, latency, jitter.

Real-World Example:

Think of passive mode like listening to a radio to see which stations come in clearly.

Think of active mode like actually calling the station to test the connection quality.

🔑 SELF-ORGANIZING WLANs

Definition: New controller-based access points, or self-organizing WLANs, provide the ability to dynamically reconfigure lightweight thin access points based on changing RF conditions.

Key Points:

  • Network can adjust:
    • Signals
    • Channels
    • Power levels
    • Patterns
  • Maintains optimal operating characteristics and network performance.

Real-World Example:

Think of self-organizing WLANs like a smart thermostat. It adjusts automatically based on conditions, without you having to manually change settings.

📝 EXAM QUESTIONS — FULL CHAPTER
QuestionAnswer (Simplified)
1. What is the fundamental requirement of WLAN communication?

All devices must be tuned to a common radio frequency within the ISM unlicensed spectrum.

2. What does ISM stand for?

Industrial, Scientific, and Medical band of unlicensed wireless spectrum.

3. What are the most common ISM bands for wireless devices?

2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz (new).

4. What is a STA?

Station — any 802.11-capable device (radio card).

5. What is an SSID?

Service Set Identifier — a configurable name for an access point.

6. What is the difference between passive and active scanning?

Passive: client listens for beacons. Active: client sends probe requests.

7. What are the 4 service sets?

BSS (Basic), ESS (Extended), IBSS (Independent), MBSS (Mesh).

8. What is the difference between infrastructure and ad hoc mode?

Infrastructure: uses an AP. Ad hoc: devices communicate directly.

9. What is Wi-Fi 6 in 802.11 terms?

802.11ax (2019).

10. What is Wi-Fi 5 in 802.11 terms?

802.11ac (2014).

11. What are the two techniques for radio transmission?

Narrowband (susceptible to jamming) and Spread Spectrum (harder to block).

12. What is FHSS?

Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum — hops between frequencies in short bursts.

13. What is DSSS?

Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum — stays on a fixed channel, hops within it using chips.

14. What is multipath?

Radio waves reflecting off surfaces, creating multiple versions of the same signal.

15. What is the difference between constructive and destructive interference?

Constructive: signals in phase → stronger. Destructive: signals out of phase → weaker/faded.

16. What techniques reduce fading?

OFDM, MIMO, Diversity, Equalization, Adaptive Modulation.

17. What is a WAP?

Wireless Access Point — a half-duplex switch with a radio card and antenna.

18. What are the non-overlapping channels in 2.4 GHz?

1, 6, and 11.

19. What is the WEP message flow?

Sender computes ICV, encrypts with RC4(IV || K), sends IV || Ciphertext. Receiver decrypts and verifies ICV.

20. What is the WPA four-way handshake?

ANonce → SNonce + MIC → Install PTK + GTK + MIC → ACK + MIC.

21. What is the PTK formula in WPA2?

PTK = PMK + ANonce + SNonce + MAC(AA) + MAC(SA).

22. What are the two types of WAP architecture?

Autonomous (intelligence in AP) and Thin (intelligence in WLAN controller).

23. What is the difference between IS and DS?

IS: redirects packets based on Layer 2 headers. DS: portal for WAPs (distribution medium + DSS).

24. What is a wireless bridge?

Uses WDS to connect WAPs and form a wireless backbone.

25. What is a workgroup bridge?

Connects non-wireless devices to a wireless domain.

26. What is the difference between residential and enterprise gateways?

Residential: ~10 connections. Enterprise: 100+ connections, WLAN + LAN interface, guest access.

27. What are the three antenna categories?

Omnidirectional (360°), Semi-directional (180°), Highly directional (narrow beam).

28. What are the two types of highly directional antennas?

Grid antenna and Parabolic antenna.

29. What is MIMO?

Multiple Input Multiple Output — multiple antennas transmit/receive concurrently.

30. What is a site survey?

Determines how RF radiation behaves within a facility (coverage, interference, gaps).

31. What is a spectrum analyzer?

Test device that measures amplitude and frequency of electromagnetic signals.

32. What are common sources of noise/interference?

Microwave ovens, cordless phones, elevator motors, Bluetooth, other networks, jammers.

33. What is the difference between passive and active coverage assessment?

Passive: collects RF measurements. Active: authenticates/associates; provides packet loss, latency, jitter.

34. What is a self-organizing WLAN?

Controller-based APs that dynamically reconfigure based on changing RF conditions.

✅ THE CORE TRUTH
WLANs work by tuning devices to common radio frequencies in the ISM bands. The 802.11 standards define how devices connect (service sets), how signals are spread (FHSS, DSSS), and how they are secured (WEP → WPA → WPA2). Access points are the central hubs, bridges extend range, and antennas shape coverage. Site surveys ensure optimal performance by analyzing spectrum, noise, and coverage before deployment.
