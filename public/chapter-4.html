CHAPTER 4: SECURITY THREATS OVERVIEW – WIRED, WIRELESS, AND MOBILE

SECTION 1: WHAT TO PROTECT? (THE FIVE ASPECTS)

🔑 THE FIVE ASPECTS OF THREAT IDENTIFICATION

Definition: Before you can secure anything, you must answer five critical questions about the asset you are protecting.

The Five Aspects:

AspectQuestionExamples
1. What are you protecting?What is the asset?Corporate data, intellectual property, financial assets, remote control of a physical device.
2. Why are you protecting it?Is it mandated or best practice?Government regulation, industry standard, internal policy.
3. What is the value of the asset?Have you quantified it?Cost of a data breach, replacement cost, reputation damage.
4. What are you protecting from?Internal or external? What type?Data theft, device control, system access, environmental, human.
5. What constraints prevent protection?What limits your options?Broad access required, data changes/moves, budget, usability.

Real-World Example:

Think of securing a bank vault. You must know: What's inside (gold, cash)? Why protect it (customer trust)? How much is it worth? Who are the threats (robbers, insiders)? What limits you (need for teller access)?

Key Takeaway:

You can't protect what you haven't defined. Answer the five aspects first.
SECTION 2: GENERAL THREAT CATEGORIES & PRINCIPLES

🔑 CORE SECURITY PRINCIPLES

Definition: Information security is built on principles that guide all security measures.

Key Points:

  • Least privilege: Give people only the rights they need to do their job—nothing more.
  • Alignment: Security must align with business objectives and asset functions.
  • Proportionality: Security measures should match the value of the asset—don't over-secure a low-value asset or under-secure a high-value one.
  • Information policy: The foundation and rationale behind all security measures.
  • The five principles of information security: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Accountability, Nonrepudiation.

Memory Aid:

"CIA + A + N" – Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Accountability, Nonrepudiation.

🔑 CONFIDENTIALITY

Definition: Ensuring information is not disclosed to unauthorized individuals.

Key Points:

  • Traditional: Securing access to information on storage devices and servers via access policies.
  • Modern network: Privacy (protecting data from being seen) + Secrecy (hiding knowledge of data's existence or whereabouts).
  • Concern: Information leaves the organization (laptops, smartphones, IoT devices).
  • IoT risk: A great deal of information can be gleaned by tracking device usage, on/off times, and settings.

Real-World Example:

Think of confidentiality like a locked diary. Only you have the key. If someone reads it, confidentiality is breached.

🔑 INTEGRITY

Definition: Assurance that information is genuine, remains true to its original form, and has not been manipulated or tampered with.

Key Points:

  • One-way functions like Hash algorithms and Message Authentication Codes (MACs) provide integrity assurance.
  • Used when data passes over unsecured networks (like the internet).

Real-World Example:

Think of integrity like a wax seal on a letter. If the seal is broken, you know someone tampered with it.

🔑 AVAILABILITY

Definition: Ensuring systems and services are available to users when needed, and not withheld by unauthorized means.

Key Points:

  • Focus: Preventing Denial of Service (DoS) attacks.
  • SYN Flood Attack: Attacker sends many fake SYN requests. Server responds with SYN-ACK, but attacker never sends ACK. Server's connection table fills up, blocking legitimate users.
  • The Three-Way Handshake (TCP):
    1. SYN: Requester sends synchronization request.
    1. SYN-ACK: Server acknowledges.
    1. ACK: Requester acknowledges.

Diagram (Three-Way Handshake):

Requester                Server
   |------- SYN -------->|
   |<----- SYN-ACK ------|
   |------- ACK -------->|

Real-World Example:

Think of a SYN flood like thousands of people calling a pizza shop and hanging up immediately. The phone lines are all busy, so real customers can't get through.

Key Takeaway:

Availability = keep services running. DoS attacks try to stop that.

🔑 ACCOUNTABILITY

Definition: The traceability of actions performed on a system to a specific entity (user, process, device).

Key Points:

  • Serious breaches can come from authorized users inside the network.
  • Key point: Authentication. A user cannot be accountable if the system hasn't authenticated them.
  • Authorization restricts access on a per-user basis.

Real-World Example:

Think of accountability like a security badge log. Every time you enter a door, it's recorded. If something goes missing, you can trace who was there.

🔑 NONREPUDIATION

Definition: Provides undeniable evidence that an action was taken and by whom—so someone cannot deny it.

Key Points:

  • Important in e-commerce and financial transactions (online trading).
  • Digital signatures provide nonrepudiation of origin.

Real-World Example:

Think of nonrepudiation like a signed contract. You can't later say, "I never signed that."
SECTION 3: THREATS TO WIRELESS AND MOBILE DEVICES

🔑 THREE SPECIFIC THREAT CATEGORIES

Definition: Threats to wireless and mobile devices fall into three main categories.

Threat CategoryWhat it TargetsExamples
Data Theft ThreatsStealing informationSniffing, malware, browser exploits, phishing, lost/stolen devices, system takeover.
Device Control ThreatsGaining control of the deviceUnauthorized clients, ad hoc connections, endpoint attacks, Bluetooth/Wi-Fi hacks, NFC/proximity hacking.
System Access ThreatsGaining access to the network/systemDoS attacks, evil twin APs, rogue APs.

Memory Aid:

"Data, Device, System" – the three D's of mobile threats.

🔑 DATA THEFT THREATS (IN DETAIL)

Definition: Hackers look for Personally Identifiable Information (PII) and business data.

Areas of Interest for Cybercriminals:

  • Credentials for personal or business accounts.
  • Credentials for business or personal information.
  • Credentials for remote access software for business networks.
  • Access to data and phone services.

Most Common Threats:

ThreatDescription
Sniffing (Snooping)Capturing data traveling over the network.
Malicious Applications (Malware)Apps designed to steal data or harm the device.
Browser ExploitsVulnerabilities in web browsers.
Wireless PhishingFake networks or messages to steal credentials.
Lost or Stolen DevicesPhysical theft leading to data access.
System or Device TakeoverGaining full control of the device.

Real-World Example:

Think of data theft like a pickpocket. They look for wallets (credentials), phones (devices), and anything valuable they can grab quickly.

🔑 DEVICE CONTROL THREATS (IN DETAIL)

Definition: Threats that aim to take control of the device or its connections.

ThreatDescription
Unauthorized and Modified ClientsUsers circumvent policies (jailbreaking, open hotspots) creating backdoors.
Ad Hoc Connections & Software-Based APsDevices connecting directly, bypassing security.
Endpoint AttacksAttacks on Wi-Fi clients.
Bluetooth/Wi-Fi HacksExploiting protocol vulnerabilities to control mobile devices.
NFC and Proximity HackingNFC pairing is automatic between credit card and PoS terminals—can be exploited.

Real-World Example:

Think of device control like someone hijacking your car. They don't just steal your stuff—they drive the car wherever they want.

🔑 SYSTEM ACCESS THREATS (IN DETAIL)

Definition: Threats that aim to gain access to the network or system.

ThreatDescription
DoS AttacksNetwork-based or radio-based attacks to make systems unavailable.
Evil Twin Access PointsFake AP with same SSID as legitimate WLAN, tricking users into connecting.
Rogue Access PointsHacker sets up an AP in a wireless dead zone to access the network.

Real-World Example:

Think of an evil twin like a fake ATM that looks exactly like your bank's ATM. You put your card in, and the thief steals your information.
SECTION 4: RISKS WITH BYOD & RISK MITIGATION

⚠️ RISKS WITH BYOD

RiskDescription
Legal separationPersonal vs. business use is hard to separate.
Data leakageCompany data can leak into the wild.
Policy enforcementHard to enforce governance on personal devices.
Loss and theftPersonal devices are easily lost or stolen.

Key Takeaway:

BYOD = convenience + risk. You must balance user satisfaction with security.

🔑 RISK MITIGATION STRATEGIES

Definition: Techniques to reduce the risks associated with mobile devices and BYOD.

Key Mitigation Strategies:

  • COPE (Corporate-Owned, Personally Enabled): Company owns the device but allows personal use.
  • Screen locks and password protection.
  • Remote locks and data wipes.
  • Mobile GPS location and tracking.
  • Stored data encryption.
  • Compliance with business plan and policy.
  • Balance user satisfaction with risk mitigation.

🔑 MITIGATING THE RISK OF BYOD: MDM & MAM

Mobile Device Management (MDM):

  • Enables network security administrators to manage mobile devices.
  • Architecture: server element (central management) + client element (resides on mobile devices).

Mobile Application Management (MAM):

  • Secure applications: Some apps can open phone features—control them.
  • Secure network access: Both user and device should be authenticated.
  • Encryption: Data should be encrypted in transport and ideally in local storage too.

Benefits:

  • Empower people.
  • Protect sensitive information.
  • Protect devices and data from unauthorized access.

Also mentioned: Desktop virtualization.

Real-World Example:

Think of MDM like a remote control for your company's devices. You can lock, wipe, or track any device from a central dashboard.

Definition: Controls are safeguards applied to networks and systems to protect assets.

Control TypeDescriptionExamples

Logical/Technical ControlsHardware and software that protect the network.Antivirus software, firewalls.

Key Point: Controls are applied in layers—if an attacker breaches one layer, others still protect the assets.

SECTION 6: AUTHORIZATION, ACCESS CONTROL & AAA

🔑 AAA PROTOCOL

Definition: The key to applying security is to allow authorized users to work unhindered while preventing unauthorized access.

The AAA Framework:

ComponentDefinitionAnalogy
AuthenticationValidating a claimed identity (user, device, application).Checking an ID card at the door.
AuthorizationGranting access rights to a user, group, system, or application.Giving a key to specific rooms.
AccountabilityChronological record of system activity for forensic examination.Security camera footage.

Context-Aware Security:

  • Provides greater granularity in applying access controls.
  • Security policy dictates the rules for authentication.

Real-World Example:

Think of AAA like a hotel: Authentication = front desk checks your ID. Authorization = your key card opens only your room and the gym. Accountability = the hotel logs every door you open.

🔑 SARBANES-OXLEY ACT (SOX) – DETAILED

Definition: Enacted to address investor confidence and corporate financial fraud through reporting standards for public companies.

Key Reporting & Control Expectations:

  • Financial reports should be accurate and complete.
  • Material information should be disclosed appropriately and on time.
  • Management is responsible for establishing and evaluating internal controls over financial reporting.
  • Records supporting financial statements must be protected from unauthorized alteration or destruction.
  • Independent auditors evaluate relevant financial reporting and control requirements.

Two Important SOX Sections:

SectionRequirement
Section 302Senior executives certify accuracy of periodic financial reports and address disclosure controls.
Section 404Management assesses internal control over financial reporting; issuers have auditor attestation requirements.

Why IT Security Matters to SOX:

  • Access controls for financial systems
  • Change management for applications/databases
  • Audit trails and logging
  • Backup, retention, and recovery
  • Separation of duties

🔑 GRAMM-LEACH-BLILEY ACT (GLBA) – DETAILED

Definition: Originally enacted in 1999 to secure and protect personally identifiable information held by financial institutions. Deals with confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

What Information is Protected? (Nonpublic Personal Information – NPI):

  • Name, address, and contact information
  • Account numbers and balances
  • Income, credit, and loan information
  • Transaction/payment history
  • Information obtained when providing a financial product or service

Three Core GLBA Concepts:

ConceptDescription
PrivacyExplain collection/sharing practices and applicable customer choices.
SafeguardsMaintain a security program to protect customer information.
Pretexting ProtectionReduce unauthorized access obtained through impersonation or deception.

Practical Security Example:

  • MFA and least-privilege access
  • Encryption and secure transmission
  • Risk assessment and security monitoring
  • Service-provider oversight and incident response

🔑 PCI DSS – DETAILED

Definition: The most pervasive industry regulation. Aims to ensure safe and secure handling of credit cardholder information at all steps of the payment process.

PCI DSS 6 Control Objectives (12 Requirements):

  1. Build and maintain a secure network.
  1. Protect cardholders.
  1. Maintain a vulnerability management program.
  1. Implement strong access control measures.
  1. Regularly monitor and test networks.
  1. Maintain an information security policy.

What is Being Protected?

  • Primary Account Number (PAN)
  • Cardholder name / expiration date
  • Sensitive authentication data such as security codes and PIN-related data

Practical Security Example:

  • Encrypt card data during transmission
  • Do not retain sensitive data unnecessarily
  • Use least privilege and MFA for administrative access
  • Log, monitor, patch, scan, and test the environment

🔑 GDPR – DETAILED

Definition: GDPR governs how organizations collect, use, share, secure, retain, and dispose of personal data, while giving individuals important rights over their information.

GDPR's 7 Key Principles:

  1. Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency: Collected on a legal basis, fair, transparent, clear usage.
  1. Purpose limitation: Agreed purpose only.
  1. Data minimization: Minimum required to fulfill the agreed purpose.
  1. Accuracy: User data must be accurate.
  1. Storage limitation: User data only stored for a specific, stated time.
  1. Integrity and confidentiality: User data must be adequately protected.
  1. Accountability: Handle user data responsibly and comply with the other principles.

Important Data-Subject Rights:

  • Access and rectification
  • Erasure and restriction (where applicable)
  • Data portability
  • Objection
  • Rights concerning certain automated decisions

Before & During Processing User Data:

  • Identify the purpose and an appropriate lawful basis.
  • Provide transparent privacy information.
  • Use appropriate technical and organizational security measures.
  • Keep only data that is necessary for as long as it is needed.

🔑 CCPA (CALIFORNIA CONSUMER PRIVACY ACT)

  • California's version of GDPR.
  • Came into force in 2020.
  • Impact on mobile apps: Developers can no longer harvest vast amounts of user data without permission or product-relative purpose.
  • Example: Wearable health monitors previously harvested user data remotely. Now they must be careful not to misuse personal data.

Real-World Example:

Think of GDPR/CCPA like a privacy bill of rights. You own your data; companies must ask permission and be transparent about how they use it.

⚠️ DETRIMENTAL EFFECTS OF REGULATIONS

ProblemExplanation
Confusion between security and complianceMany regulations address business requirements, not security standards.
Compliance is a subset of securityNot the overriding goal.
Security best practices + regulatory compliance = Corporate Security.Both are needed.
Bandwagon effectCities create their own regulations, causing confusion.
Regulation vs. innovationRegulations can slow down innovation.
Auditor can only audit what is documentedIf it's not written down, it doesn't count.

Key Takeaway:

Compliance ≠ Security. Compliance is a baseline; security is the goal.

📝 EXAM QUESTIONS — FULL CHAPTER
QuestionAnswer (Simplified)
1. What are the five aspects of threat identification?

What protect? Why protect? Value? Protect from? Constraints?

2. What are the five principles of information security?

Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability, Accountability, Nonrepudiation.

3. What is the difference between confidentiality and integrity?

Confidentiality = prevent unauthorized viewing. Integrity = prevent unauthorized modification.

4. What is a SYN flood attack?

Attacker sends many fake SYN requests; server responds with SYN-ACK, but attacker never sends ACK. Server's connection table fills up.

5. What is the three-way handshake?

SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK. (1) Requester asks to connect. (2) Server acknowledges. (3) Requester confirms.

6. What is nonrepudiation?

Undeniable evidence that an action was taken and by whom (digital signatures).

7. What are the three threat categories for wireless/mobile?

Data theft, device control, system access.

8. What is an evil twin AP?

Fake access point with same SSID as legitimate WLAN, tricking users into connecting.

9. What is the difference between COPE and BYOD?

COPE = company owns device, allows personal use. BYOD = employee owns device, uses for work.

10. What is MDM?

Mobile Device Management: central server + client on devices to manage and secure them.

11. What is defense in depth?

Multiple layers of security so an attacker must breach several to reach the target.

12. Name the 6 layers of defense in depth (outer to inner).

Policies/Awareness → Physical → Perimeter → LAN → Host → Application.

13. What are the three types of security controls?

Physical, logical/technical, administrative.

14. What is AAA?

Authentication, Authorization, Accountability.

15. What is ISO/IEC 27001?

Standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and improving an ISMS. Uses PDCA cycle.

16. What are the PDCA steps?

Plan, Do, Check, Act.

17. What is ISO/IEC 27002?

Guidelines for information security, with 14 general groups.

18. What is NIST SP 800-53?

Risk management framework for federal information systems.

19. What is SOX?

Sarbanes-Oxley Act: investor confidence, financial fraud, integrity of financial data.

20. What are the two important SOX sections?

Section 302 (executive certification) and Section 404 (internal control assessment).

21. Why does IT security matter to SOX?

Access controls, change management, audit trails, backup/recovery, separation of duties.

22. What is the SOX transaction flow?

Business Transaction → Financial System → Internal Controls → Financial Statements → Management/Audit.

23. What is GLBA?

Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act: protect PII held by financial institutions.

24. What are the three core GLBA concepts?

Privacy, Safeguards, Pretexting protection.

25. What is the GLBA data flow?

Customer → Bank/Lender → Core/Cloud System → Employee/App → Service Provider.

26. What is HIPAA?

Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act: privacy and security for patients.

27. What is PHI?

Protected Health Information: health info linked to an identifiable individual.

28. What is the HIPAA Security Rule?

Protect ePHI through administrative, physical, and technical safeguards.

29. What is the HIPAA/HITECH data flow?

Patient → Hospital/Clinic → EHR System → Doctor/Lab/Pharmacy → Insurer/Partner.

30. What is PCI DSS?

Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard: 12 requirements, 6 control objectives.

31. What is being protected under PCI DSS?

Primary Account Number (PAN), cardholder name/expiration, sensitive authentication data.

32. What is the PCI DSS transaction flow?

Cardholder → Merchant/POS → Acquirer/Processor → Card Network → Issuing Bank.

33. What is GDPR?

General Data Protection Regulation: 7 key principles for user data protection.

34. What are the 7 GDPR principles?

Lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity/confidentiality, accountability.

35. What is the GDPR personal-data lifecycle?

Collect → Purpose & Lawful Basis → Inform → Process & Secure → Share/Transfer → Rights & Retention → Delete/Anonymize.

36. What is the 72-hour rule?

GDPR breach notification must be made within 72 hours of awareness.

37. What is the GDPR breach response flow?

Detect & Contain → Investigate → Assess Risk → Notify/Document → Remediate.

38. What is CCPA?

California Consumer Privacy Act: California's version of GDPR.

39. What is the detrimental effect of regulations?

Confusion between security and compliance; compliance is a subset, not the goal.

✅ THE CORE TRUTH
Security is not just about technology—it's about defining what you're protecting, understanding the threats, and applying layered defenses. Standards (ISO, NIST) and regulations (SOX, GLBA, HIPAA, PCI DSS, GDPR, CCPA) provide frameworks, but compliance is only a subset of true security. The goal is to balance protection with usability, and to never stop improving.
